## State Requirements

Washington DC is a federal district rather than a state, but plumbers and gasfitters are regulated locally through the **District of Columbia Board of Industrial Trades**.

Under current District law, an applicant for licensure as a **journeyman plumber or journeyman gasfitter** may qualify by having worked as an apprentice plumber or gasfitter for at least **8,000 hours over at least four years**.

The District also recognizes an education-based pathway for an applicant who has graduated from an accredited college or university with a **degree in mechanical engineering** and has at least **two years of practical experience as a plumber or gasfitter** verified by a licensed Master Plumber or licensed Master Gasfitter.

The Board may also recognize **comparable experience or a combination of education and experience** that it determines to be equivalent to the established pathways. Additional evidence may be required for a particular specialty license.

District law also recognizes a national-certification pathway. The Board may accept a qualifying certificate from a recognized national certifying organization when the applicant has completed the organization's apprenticeship program, passed its examination, holds the organization's journeyman designation, and remains in good standing under the applicable requirements.

The legal scope of plumbing in Washington DC includes installing, repairing, maintaining, and removing plumbing fixtures and piping associated with water supply and drainage systems. Gasfitting includes work involving nonindustrial gas appliances and piping systems from the applicable meter or service-regulator outlet to appliance connections.

Candidates pursuing a combined Journeyman Plumber with Gas credential should therefore prepare for both plumbing-system and fuel-gas responsibilities rather than studying only one discipline.

An individual journeyman trade license is also different from a **Plumbing and Gasfitting Contractor license**. A contracting business must separately satisfy the business licensing and contractor requirements that apply in the District.

## Test Information and Study Materials

**Plumbing Fixtures and Terminology** account for 5% of the examination. Review plumbing definitions, fixture classifications, installation requirements, fixture connections, and terminology used throughout the International Plumbing Code.

Definitions are important because code questions often depend on precise terminology. Candidates should understand the difference between commonly confused plumbing terms and know where definitions can be located quickly.

**Plumbing Fundamentals** represent 13%. Study basic plumbing principles, general code requirements, piping materials, system operation, installation practices, calculations, and relationships among drainage, venting, water supply, fixtures, and traps.

**Traps, Cleanouts, and Interceptors** account for 4%. Review trap requirements, trap seals, prohibited configurations, cleanout placement, accessibility, interceptors, separators, and related plumbing provisions.

**Special Requirements** represent another 4%. These questions may address plumbing conditions or systems requiring additional code provisions beyond ordinary water, waste, and vent arrangements.

**Drainage Requirements** represent **14%**. Candidates should review drainage fixture units, pipe sizing, horizontal and vertical drainage piping, building drains, building sewers, slopes, changes in direction, connections, and applicable sizing tables.

Drainage calculations deserve repeated practice. Candidates should be able to determine fixture-unit loads and select an appropriate pipe size from the correct IPC table.

**Venting Requirements** also represent **14%**. Study individual vents, common vents, wet venting, circuit venting, vent sizing, vent connections, terminal requirements, and the relationship between traps and the venting system.

Plumbing venting should not be confused with fuel-gas appliance venting. Both are covered on this combined examination, but they are governed by different provisions and appear in different code books.

**Water Distribution** accounts for 12%. Review water-service piping, fixture supplies, water-distribution sizing, valves, pressure, backflow protection, water heaters, and potable-water system requirements.

Water-distribution questions may involve sizing, pressure, materials, system layout, or protection against contamination. Candidates should practice navigating between tables and the explanatory code sections surrounding them.

**Plan Reading** represents **14%**. Candidates should be comfortable interpreting plumbing diagrams, identifying fixtures and piping, tracing drainage and vent systems, understanding water-distribution layouts, and applying code requirements to drawings.

## Test Information and Study Materials (continued)

A productive plan-reading strategy is to analyze one system at a time. Trace sanitary drainage first, then vents, water distribution, and gas piping. Breaking the drawing into systems can make complex plans easier to interpret.

**Gas** is the largest examination category at **20%**. Candidates should devote substantial study time to the 2012 International Fuel Gas Code.

Review gas-piping materials, system installation, pipe sizing, shutoff valves, appliance connections, pressure considerations, combustion air, equipment installation, testing, and fuel-gas venting.

Gas-pipe sizing is an important calculation area. Practice selecting the correct table and using system length, connected demand, pressure, and other applicable factors to determine the required piping size.

Combustion air is another important subject. Candidates should understand the requirements for supplying adequate combustion, ventilation, and dilution air to fuel-burning appliances and know how to locate the applicable sizing methods.

Fuel-gas appliance venting should also receive dedicated attention. Review vent connectors, chimney and vent requirements, termination provisions, clearances, sizing, and appliance installation requirements.

The exam allows **four hours for 70 questions**, so candidates should use practice sessions to develop a consistent pace. Knowing which reference applies to a question before beginning the lookup can save significant time.

When a practice question is missed, identify the reason. The problem may involve selecting the wrong code book, choosing the wrong sizing table, misreading a plan, overlooking an exception, using the wrong fixture-unit value, or confusing plumbing venting with fuel-gas venting.

Repeated work with both approved references helps candidates build familiarity with code organization and table locations. Over time, common plumbing and gas subjects should become easier to find without broad searching.
