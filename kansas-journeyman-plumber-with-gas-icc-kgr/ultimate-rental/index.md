## Test Information and Study Materials

The most effective open-book preparation is performance-based. Reading has value, but readiness improves fastest when you repeatedly train the same actions you’ll use under time pressure: identify the topic, choose IPC vs. IFGC, confirm the controlling language, and maintain steady pacing.

**1) Master the first-book decision (IPC vs. IFGC)**
With two code books, the first move matters. Before opening a book, decide which reference is most likely controlling the answer. During practice, make this a rule: label the topic, choose your starting book, then locate and confirm. If you start in the wrong place, pause and identify what clue in the question should have sent you to the other book first. This is how speed and accuracy grow together.

**2) Learn to confirm instead of reread**
A common time trap is rereading long sections when one sentence decides the answer. Train a confirm-and-move habit. When you locate a section, scan for language that changes what applies:

- **Conditions** that define when a rule applies
- **Exceptions** that modify or remove a general rule
- **Definitions** that change what the scenario means
- **Requirement language** that signals what must be done

This approach keeps your lookups efficient and helps you avoid “almost correct” answers that miss a qualifier or exception.

**3) Build memory of location through repeated lookups**
Open-book exams reward familiarity. You build familiarity by repeatedly locating common categories of information. Each successful lookup strengthens your sense of where similar requirements live and how each code organizes related topics. Over time, you’ll notice you’re faster—not because you’re guessing better, but because you’ve practiced the same navigation patterns again and again.

**4) Practice pacing like it’s part of the skill**
Open book doesn’t mean unlimited time. Pacing is part of readiness. During timed practice, avoid turning one question into a long research project. Confirm what you can efficiently, choose the best supported answer, and move forward. If time allows, you can return—protecting your overall momentum.
